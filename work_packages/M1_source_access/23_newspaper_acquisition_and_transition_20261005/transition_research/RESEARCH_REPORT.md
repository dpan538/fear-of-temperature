# Newspaper and Social-Media News Use: Country-Specific Transition Evidence

Prepared 5 October 2026. This is a bounded background review for *Fear of Temperature*, with 12 core evidence families and two supplementary scholarly sources. It contains 25 claim-level evidence rows, 53 published country-wave comparisons and five verified journal references. The review examines audience use, newspaper formats, distribution and agenda flow. It makes no claim of exhaustive literature coverage or completed source acquisition.

There is no defensible single year when social media replaced newspapers across the five study strata. Published turning points depend on whether the question asks about past-week use, frequent use, a single main source, print alone or newspaper brands including digital editions. A change in the ordering of two rounded survey percentages is an observed comparison, not evidence of complete substitution, statistical equivalence, a permanent change or a monthly transition date. Newspaper publishers can also supply the news encountered on social platforms.

The following comparisons preserve those distinctions. Source links lead to original producers; evidence IDs map to the detailed table. “By” means the cited wave already shows an ordering but the earliest crossing was not established. An interval brackets survey observations; it does not assume a monotonic change between them.

These survey series should remain separate. Reuters uses weighted, non-probability online panels and filters respondents without news use in the preceding month. That is an external survey eligibility rule, not a proposed corpus exclusion. Pew's cited 2018 result comes from the American Trends Panel survey of 3,425 US adults, fielded 30 July–12 August. Ofcom includes offline respondents through mixed modes and uses a multiple-response “nowadays” question. Its figure caption “main platforms” does not mean respondents selected only one main source. Reuters Q4, in contrast, conditions its single-main-source percentages on respondents who used a source in the preceding week. [Reuters methodology 2015](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/archive/survey/2015/survey-methodology-2015/index.html), [Pew linked survey](https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/2018/12/03/americans-still-prefer-watching-to-reading-the-news-and-mostly-still-through-television/)

Age variation needs its own denominator. Pew's 2018 social/print comparison for 18–29-year-olds is 36% versus 2%; this is a cross-sectional subgroup result, not the all-adult crossover or a demonstrated cohort effect. A source's audience composition also need not match the ages of authors available in a text corpus. [Pew 2018](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2018/12/10/social-media-outpaces-print-newspapers-in-the-u-s-as-a-news-source/)

New Zealand illustrates why a gap should remain visible. NZ On Air separates social posts from news media, friends/family and community groups, while naming individual news brands and a residual newspaper category. Overlapping response percentages cannot be added into a social-media union or compared with one newspaper brand as if it represented the sector. The report's broader daily-media questions also do not supply a news-specific crossover. The New Zealand uncertainty is a limitation of the recovered comparative evidence, not evidence of zero public expression or failed acquisition. [NZ On Air 2024](https://www.nzonair.govt.nz/documents/1183/Where_are_the_Audiences_2024_Report_Final_21_08_24.pdf)

Scholarly work supports several mechanisms without identifying one common switch year. Westlund and Färdigh examine Swedish evening-tabloid repertoires across annual surveys from 1986 to 2012 and describe both displacement and complementarity across print, computer and mobile formats. Only their original abstract was accessible here; no social-media crossover or unverified effect size is extracted. The Dutch two-wave study by de Waal and Schoenbach concerns news websites in 2002 and 2005, and therefore provides historical format context rather than evidence of platform adoption. [Westlund and Färdigh, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/2050157914549039), [de Waal and Schoenbach, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444809341859)

Fletcher and Nielsen's accepted manuscript uses 2015 online-panel data from Italy, Australia, the UK and US. People incidentally encountering news on social media report more online news sources than non-users; matching and regressions address observed differences, but the cross-sectional design does not establish causality. This supports an overlap-aware design: accessing a newspaper through a platform does not turn the original newspaper article into an independently authored public utterance. The journal issue year is 2018; online publication was in 2017. [Fletcher and Nielsen](https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444817724170)

Thurman and Fletcher's longitudinal case of *The Independent* separates reach from attention around its March 2016 print cessation. Monthly British readership rose 7.7%, while total reading time fell 81% in the first post-print year. This documented brand transition is useful for edition and format metadata; it is neither an all-newspaper result nor a social-media crossover. Circulation copies, deduplicated people, reading minutes and corpus records have different denominators. [Thurman and Fletcher, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/21670811.2018.1504625)

Cornia and colleagues study selected newspapers and commercial broadcasters through 21 interviews in six European countries during April–May 2018, alongside account analytics. Referral traffic, off-site reach and subscriptions are distinct organizational aims. Such organizational evidence explains why platform and publisher channels overlap, but cannot calibrate national text volumes. Neuman and colleagues' original abstract describes interacting traditional/social issue attention in 2012. Agenda flow is a separate construct from audience choice; no detailed lag estimates or causal emotion claims are taken from the abstract-only source. [Cornia et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.60625/risj-cg3f-he14), [Neuman et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcom.12088)

Several provenance and comparability issues remain explicit in the tables. The 2015 Reuters UK historical values differ from the 2016–2018 report vintages; Germany's 2020 Print series also differs from the 2015 vintage. No values were silently replaced or averaged. UK 2017 Print rises to 41% in both the embedded data and original PDF plot; its explanation remains unresolved. Ofcom's 2024 Figure 1 and methods report different sample totals. Its appendix describes telephone-plus-online interviewing in 2021, despite an overview statement implying no fieldwork; that year is not used in the comparable series. New online-newspaper questions in 2023 and the placement of YouTube under social media in 2024 also require sensitivity checks. NZ On Air's background and figure bases differ without an explanation verified here. Australia 2025 is supported by the original release and verified report metadata, with its inaccessible full-report methods left pending. These limitations concern the particular comparisons, not the truth of every recorded statement or a ranking of official versus public expression.

For the project's next design decisions, use the evidence as diagnostic context and retain the following constraints:

- Preserve the fixed publication interval **1988-01-01 through 2026-09-21**, its 465 calendar months and partial final month. Survey/report publication dates are separate from their fieldwork dates, retrieval times and the corpus endpoint.
- Maintain independent newspaper and social source frames, manifests, native units and coverage denominators. Verify title/edition classification; broadcaster and digital commentary sources retain their evidenced genres. Determine discourse role from author and publishing function. Editorial or official platform accounts do not automatically enter the public/civic frame.
- Register each source's evidenced existence, accessible interface and recoverable era. Pre-existence months are structurally inapplicable; missing or inaccessible applicable months remain gaps or unknowns. A platform launch is not an audience crossover or proof of retrievable posts. Historical letters/forums remain distinct public-expression subframes.
- Before interpreting a count change, inspect source, language, genre and independent article/post parent counts; pagination, duplicate IDs/URLs, syndication, mirrored editions, versions, attachments, batch boundaries and date assignment. External audience windows are candidate checkpoints only; they do not establish the cause of a corpus peak or identify a climate event.
- Prepare later comparability checks by report vintage, source language, publication format, platform carrier and audience definition. Specific-country surveys do not make a representative pan-European measure, and national-language samples do not certify an English-language European corpus. Audience shares, referral traffic and engagement supply no automatic record weights, equal-volume collection quotas or sampling exclusions.
- Keep later climate retrieval/similarity validation, affect/risk association and fear-specific interpretation separate. This review runs no topic/emotion labels, no fear-series inclusion test and no semantic cleaning. Later RQ1/RQ2 lead/lag results remain descriptive or predictive unless identification requirements are met.

The package is ready for coordinator review as background evidence and a design aid. It changes no proposal, frozen pilot, collection release condition or shared project baseline. It performs no newspaper/social corpus acquisition or government-data work. The recommendation table is a bounded set of structural checks; it does not authorize downstream repair, backfill or social-platform collection.
