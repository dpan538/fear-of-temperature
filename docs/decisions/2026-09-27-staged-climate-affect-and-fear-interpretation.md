## Decision

The thesis continues to investigate fear of rising temperature as a social and historical question. The early computational work does **not** map fear directly. It first assembles dated climate/warming discourse across government, media and civic/public sources, then examines macro-level associations with affective language, anticipated harm, risk and responsibility. Fear-specific interpretation and attribution follow only after evidence-linked passage review and construct validation.

This distinguishes three claims that cannot be interchanged:

| Stage | What it can establish | What it cannot establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Topic/source coverage | A dated, attributable text exists and may discuss climate or warming | That the author expressed fear, or that a population felt fear |
| Similarity and macro association | Climate-related language occurs near or changes alongside affective, risk or future-oriented language under a defined source frame | A calibrated fear score, an emotion holder, or a causal response |
| Passage interpretation | With original wording and context, a reviewed expression may be classified as fear, worry, anxiety, grief, direct heat danger, quoted emotion or a non-emotional risk claim; its target/horizon/attribution can be described | A population prevalence or policy/media causal effect without representative data and further validation |

## Why this matters now

The Paris readiness pilot established government text presence in 49/49 months, a Guardian December candidate inventory and a bounded published-petition frame. Those are source-readiness observations. The diagnostic samples' zero explicit-fear labels do not make the sources irrelevant and do not demonstrate absence of fear. Conversely, a climate title, anticipated-harm cue, vector similarity or an institutional warning cannot be silently promoted to an observed fear expression. Government risk language, media quotation and civic authorship have different speakers and evidentiary meaning.

RQ1 and RQ2 therefore start with documented climate attention and, where validated, affect/risk associations across roles and event windows. They may proceed with descriptive comparisons before a comparable emotional outcome exists. RQ3 later uses similarity groupings and original passages to interpret how fear is expressed and to whom responsibility or harm is attributed. Discourse attribution is distinct from causal attribution in a temporal model.

## Execution consequences

- Do not require `fear`, `anxiety` or anticipated-harm terms for source acquisition or climate-relevance retrieval. Keep neutral and routine climate records within the defined source frame.
- Keep climate topic, affective/risk association, and explicit fear as separate fields or review states; missing evidence is not a zero emotion value.
- Inspect quotation, negation, speaker/holder, target and temporal horizon before interpreting an expression as fear.
- Do not call the 396 Guardian candidates climate articles, emotion observations or a fear numerator; do not treat the 2015 petition query as a representative public denominator.
- A pilot with no explicit fear may still establish source feasibility or broader climate-affect associations. Escalate to fear-specific quantification only if annotation and cross-source checks justify it.
- Preserve the submitted proposal and the round-1/round-2 Paris pilot as historical snapshots. Apply this decision to new work, project guidance and future reporting.
